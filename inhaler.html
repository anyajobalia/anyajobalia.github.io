Engineering Project / Product Prototyping / 2025

Discreet Asthma Inhaler.

A connected medical device prototype exploring discreet asthma inhaler design, inhalation sensing, and on-device feedback.

Product DesignElectronicsPressure FeedbackPrototyping
Enclosure CAD · SolidWorks
01 / Project Overview

Why we designed it.

Our six-person KGI team wanted to address two problems: some people feel uncomfortable using an inhaler in public, and it can be hard to tell whether they've inhaled correctly. We designed a compact, one-handed concept with a less medical-looking exterior, a pressure sensor, and an on-device display.

We worked through sketches, system architecture, component selection, assembly planning, and proposed tests. The result was an engineering prototype, not a clinically validated drug-delivery device.

02 / Design & Integration

System architecture.

Our proposed architecture used a pressure sensor to detect inhalation, a microcontroller to process the input, and an actuator concept for dose release. An OLED screen and indicators were included for user feedback.

Physical design

Planned space for the electronics, actuation components, and display within the enclosure.

Electronic feedback

Mapped the pressure sensor, microcontroller, and proposed solenoid actuation.

User interface

Designed the intended dose-count and status display, with additional visual and tactile feedback in the concept.

03 / Prototyping

Component integration.

We designed the enclosure around a mouthpiece, medication cartridge, rechargeable battery, OLED screen, and the proposed sensing and actuation components. The electronics layout was planned around a microcontroller reading inhalation pressure and controlling the actuator.

We prepared a bill of materials, assembly instructions, and a design-for-manufacture-and-assembly review. The photos show the assembled enclosure and working display, but don't establish that the device delivered medication accurately.

04 / Engineering Decisions

Assembly and test planning.

We also looked at how long the device would take to assemble and which steps could be simplified. The DFMA review identified hand-soldering, alignment, and installing separate indicators as potential bottlenecks. A future version could use an integrated PCB and housing features that make parts easier to locate during assembly.

We set targets for cartridge fit, dose consistency, size, battery life, and durability, and outlined how those requirements could be tested. These were proposed criteria, not measured results.

05 / Reflection

What this project taught me.

I liked working on a product where the physical design and electronics had to fit together. A change to the enclosure could affect component placement, the display, or how someone held the device. The project made those tradeoffs tangible, especially when we started thinking through assembly rather than just the concept.
