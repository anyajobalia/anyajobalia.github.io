Well Plate Cell Culture Pump.

CAD, fabrication, and testing for a magnetically driven cell-culture perfusion pump built by our five-person senior design team.

CAD & Mechanical Assembly3D PrintingDesign IterationFluid TestingData Analysis
01 / Project Overview

The pump concept.

Our UC Riverside senior design team developed a small magnetic pump to circulate culture media through a six-well plate and an attached microfluidic device. We wanted to reduce the tubing and additional fluid volume associated with larger external pumping setups.

My role centered on the mechanical build and experimental work, while the pump architecture and actuation approach were team decisions.

This was a student research prototype. Flow and pressure were measured; cell viability and sterile operation were not validated.

02 / Mechanical Engineering

Mechanical design and assembly.

A fan-driven magnetic field beneath the plate rotates an impeller inside the media reservoir. The impeller moves fluid through the microfluidic device and back into the reservoir, allowing circulation without an external pump.

The exploded CAD view shows the interfaces I worked on: the well plate, pump housing, impeller, and support structure. Those interfaces mattered when translating the model into printable, assembleable parts.

03 / Changing Requirements & Iteration

Adapting the design as requirements changed.

A third of the way through the project, we were asked to incorporate a microfluidic chip. None of us had worked with microfluidics before, so we researched how it worked and revisited our design to accommodate it while staying on schedule.

We first tried driving the impeller with solenoid coils, but the rotation wasn't consistent. We switched to a fan-driven magnetic setup, and I helped revise the mechanical design for the new configuration.

Testing gave us a practical way to spot mechanical issues and decide what needed another design pass.

The final prototype combined a DC fan and neodymium magnets with an Arduino-based speed controller and MOSFET.

04 / Prototype Testing

Flow and pressure characterization.

To evaluate the prototype, we compared flow rates across fan speeds and monitored pressure over time, both with and without the added fluidic resistance.

Flow characterization

Across the tested rotational speeds, the pump alone produced 2.89–17.03 mL/min. With the microfluidic device connected, flow rates were 2.70–10.92 mL/min.

Pressure stability

At 1,350 RPM, measured hydrostatic pressure initially reached approximately 300–400 Pa and remained relatively stable for about two hours before declining toward zero by six hours. Potential causes included magnetic misalignment and inconsistent impeller rotation, highlighting the need for further durability testing.

Results summarized from the team's Medtronic BMES Student Design Competition submission. Pressure was estimated from measured water-column height using P = ρgh.

05 / Reflection

What this project taught me.

This project changed how I think about a successful prototype. An assembly can look right in CAD and still behave unpredictably once it's running. The most valuable part for me was learning to connect mechanical decisions to test data, recognize when a promising result wasn't repeatable enough, and be clear about what we hadn't validated. I also learned how much progress a team can make when everyone is willing to tackle unfamiliar engineering problems together.
